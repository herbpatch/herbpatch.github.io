Growing basics

What applies to all ten herbs: a cheap seed-starting setup, the three germination tricks, seedling care, a sowing calendar, harvest and drying — and which herbs you should never feed.

Printable version of the whole guide: free PDF, 34 pages.

Chapter 1

A seed-starting setup on a budget

Six of the ten herbs are best started indoors. You do not need a greenhouse — a shelf, a lamp and some recycled containers are enough.

Containers

Anything 2–3 inches deep with holes in the bottom: yogurt cups, egg cartons, cut-down milk cartons, or a plastic seed tray. Wash reused containers in hot soapy water — dirty pots spread seedling disease.

Seed-starting mix

Do not use garden soil; it packs down and carries disease. Buy a bag of seed-starting mix, or make your own:

Homemade seed mix
1 part coconut coir or peat moss · 1 part perlite or vermiculite · 1 part sifted, well-finished compost. Moisten it until it feels like a wrung-out sponge before you fill the pots.

Light

A bright south-facing window works for sturdy seedlings in late spring, but in late winter it is too dim and seedlings stretch. A cheap LED shop light is the single best upgrade: hang it 2–4 inches above the seedlings, raise it as they grow, and run it 14–16 hours a day on a plug-in timer.

Warmth

Most of these seeds germinate best at 60–70°F (16–21°C) — normal room temperature. The top of a fridge or a seedling heat mat speeds up slow starters like lavender.

Shopping list for the setup

  • Seed-starting mix or the homemade recipe above
  • Containers or a tray with a clear lid
  • LED shop light + timer (optional but worth it)
  • Spray bottle, plus labels — chamomile, feverfew and yarrow seedlings look alike for a month
  • Zip-lock bags and paper towels (for the cold treatment)
Chapter 2

Three tricks that make or break germination

Most failures come from treating every seed the same way. Three groups need special handling.

1. Seeds that need light

Chamomile, yarrow, feverfew, lavender, evening primrose. These seeds are tiny and germinate only when light reaches them. If you bury them, they stay asleep.

  1. Fill the pot with moist mix and firm it gently.
  2. Sprinkle the seeds on top. For dust-fine chamomile, mix the seed with a spoon of dry sand first so you can see where it lands.
  3. Press them in with a flat surface — do not cover.
  4. Mist the surface and cover the pot with a clear lid or plastic wrap until they sprout, so the surface does not dry out.

2. Seeds that need a fake winter

Echinacea, marshmallow, lavender. In nature these seeds sit through a cold, damp winter before they sprout. You can copy that in the fridge — gardeners call it cold stratification.

  1. Dampen a paper towel (damp, not dripping) or a handful of moist sand.
  2. Spread the seeds on it, fold it, put it in a zip-lock bag. Write the herb and the date on the bag.
  3. Keep it in the fridge (not the freezer): 2–4 weeks for marshmallow, 3–4 weeks for lavender, about 4 weeks for echinacea.
  4. Sow straight from the fridge. Plant any that sprout early right away.
The lazy option: sow these three outdoors in late autumn or early winter. Winter does the cold treatment for you and they come up in spring.

3. The seed that hates moving

California poppy grows a single long taproot and usually dies if transplanted. Always sow it where it will grow — in the garden bed or in its final pot. Chicory has a taproot too; sow it directly as well.

Chapter 3

From seedling to garden

Watering seedlings

Water from below: stand the pots in a tray of water for 10–20 minutes, then let them drain. The mix should stay moist, never soggy. Overwatering is the main cause of “damping off” — seedlings that suddenly fall over at the soil line.

Stop damping off: clean containers, fresh seed mix, water from below, a small fan on low for air movement, and take the lid off as soon as seedlings appear.

Thinning

When seedlings have their first pair of true leaves (the second pair to appear), keep the strongest one per pot and snip the others at soil level with scissors. Pulling them disturbs the roots of the one you keep.

Potting up

Move seedlings to a bigger pot (3–4 in) when roots start to show at the drainage holes. Hold them by a leaf, never by the stem.

Hardening off — do not skip it

Seedlings raised indoors burn and wilt if they go straight outside. Get them used to sun and wind over about a week:

DayWhere
1–21–2 hours outside in shade, out of wind
3–43–4 hours, some morning sun
5–6Most of the day outside, bring in at night
7Out day and night if no frost is forecast; plant out on a cloudy day or in the evening

Planting out

Dig a hole slightly bigger than the root ball, set the plant at the same depth it was growing, firm the soil and water well. Mulch around (not against) the stems once the soil is warm.

Chapter 4

Your sowing calendar

Everything is timed from your average last frost date — the date after which frost is unlikely in spring. In the US, search “last frost date” with your ZIP code; elsewhere, check your national weather service or a local garden centre.

Chapter 5

Harvest, drying and storage

When to pick

Harvest on a dry morning, after the dew has gone and before the midday heat. Leaves and flowers picked then hold the most aroma.

MarshmallowLeaves, flowers; rootSummer; root in autumn of year 2
LavenderFlower spikesAbout half the buds open

Three ways to dry

  • Bundles: 5–10 stems tied with a rubber band (it tightens as stems shrink), hung upside down in a dark, airy room. Slip a paper bag with holes over flowers that drop seed or petals. 1–3 weeks.
  • Screens: flower heads (chamomile, calendula) in one layer on a window screen or a baking rack lined with paper, in the shade. Turn every few days. 1–2 weeks.
  • Dehydrator: 95–115°F (35–46°C) for leaves and flowers; up to 125°F (52°C) for chopped roots. Hours instead of weeks. Avoid the oven — even the lowest setting drives off most of the aroma.

Roots

Scrub them, then chop into small pieces while still fresh — dried roots are as hard as wood.

Is it dry?

Leaves and petals crumble; stems snap; root pieces break cleanly. If in doubt, dry longer — damp herbs go mouldy in the jar.

Storage

Glass jars with tight lids, labelled with the herb and date, in a dark cupboard. Check the jars after a week: any mist on the glass means the herbs need more drying. Most dried herbs are best within a year.

Bonus

The herb garden feeding plan

Here is the rule most gardeners get wrong: most of these herbs should not be fed. They come from dry hillsides and poor meadows. Rich soil and fertiliser give them soft, leafy growth, fewer flowers and floppy stems, and many growers find lean-grown herbs more aromatic.

Never feedLight feedingLikes feeding
Lavender, yarrow, California poppy, evening primrose, echinacea, chicory, chamomileCalendula, feverfew — compost in springMarshmallow — compost in spring, liquid feed in poor soil

Seedlings: the only time everyone gets fed

Seed mix has few nutrients. Once seedlings have two true leaves, feed every 1–2 weeks with any balanced liquid fertiliser or fish emulsion at half the label strength, until they go outside.

Three homemade liquid feeds

1. Nettle tea (nitrogen)

  1. Wearing gloves, fill a bucket loosely with young nettle leaves and stems.
  2. Cover with water, put a lid on, and leave for 1–2 weeks, stirring every few days. It will smell strongly — keep it away from the house.
  3. Strain. Dilute 1 part tea to 10 parts water and pour it on the soil around marshmallow or calendula. For seedlings, 1 to 20.

2. Comfrey tea (potassium, for flowers)

Same method with comfrey leaves, 3–5 weeks. Dilute 1 to 15 and use on flowering calendula and marshmallow.

3. Compost steep

A shovel of well-finished compost in a bucket of water for 1–3 days, stirred now and then. Dilute to the colour of weak tea. Pour it on the soil, not on leaves you plan to harvest.

Willow water: root lavender cuttings for free

Lavender from seed is slow. From one plant you can root dozens of cuttings:

  1. Soak a handful of chopped young willow twigs in a jar of water for 1–2 days. The water carries natural substances that help cuttings root; gardeners have used it for generations. (Or use a store-bought rooting powder.)
  2. In late spring or early summer, cut 3–4 in (8–10 cm) non-flowering shoot tips. Strip the leaves from the bottom half.
  3. Stand the cuttings in willow water for a few hours, then push them into pots of half seed mix, half sand.
  4. Keep them bright but out of direct sun, under a clear bag, lightly moist. Roots form in 3–6 weeks — a gentle tug meets resistance.
Wood ash: lavender likes neutral to alkaline soil. In acidic soil, a light dusting of wood ash (from untreated wood) around lavender once a year does the same job as garden lime. Never use it near acid-loving plants.
