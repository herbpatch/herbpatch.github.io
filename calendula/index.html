How to grow calendula from seed

The easiest one to start with: big curved seeds, fast flowers, and it blooms until frost.

EasyAnnualCalendula officinalis · pot marigold · Daisy family (Asteraceae)

At a glance

Life cycle
Annual
Difficulty
Easy
Sun
Full sun; light afternoon shade in hot summers
Soil
Average, well-drained
Sowing depth
¼–½ in (6–12 mm) — cover the seeds, they germinate in darkness
Germination
7–14 days at 60–70°F (16–21°C)
When to sow
Direct sow in early spring, or indoors 6–8 weeks before last frost
Spacing
8–12 in (20–30 cm)
Height
12–24 in (30–60 cm)
Flowers
About 45–60 days after sowing

Step by step

  1. Sow outdoors once the hardest frosts are over — calendula shrugs off a light frost. Push each seed ¼–½ inch into the soil and cover it.
  2. Keep the soil evenly moist until seedlings appear, usually within one to two weeks.
  3. Thin seedlings to 8–12 inches apart. Crowded plants get mildew.
  4. Calendula loves cool weather and may pause in midsummer heat. Sow a second round in late summer for autumn flowers.

Watering

About an inch of water a week. Water the soil, not the leaves, in the morning; let the top inch dry between waterings. Wet leaves in humid weather invite powdery mildew.

Growing in pots

Grows well in pots: one plant per 8–10 in (20–25 cm) pot, or three in a 12 in (30 cm) pot. Any pot with drainage holes.

Feeding

Compost dug in at planting is enough. Extra nitrogen gives big leaves and fewer flowers.

Problems and fixes

  • Powdery mildew (white dust on leaves) — give plants space, water at the base, pick off affected leaves.
  • Aphids on buds — knock them off with a strong spray of water.
  • Slugs on young seedlings — check at dusk, use a beer trap or pick them off.

Harvest and drying

Pick flower heads when fully open, every two or three days — the more you pick, the more it blooms. Dry them on a screen out of direct sun; the petals feel sticky with resin, that is normal.

Storing

Dried flower heads or petals in a sealed glass jar, away from light. Best used within a year.

Saving seed

Let a few heads dry on the plant. The curled, C-shaped seeds are ready when they turn brown and fall off easily.

A bit of history

It was called “pot marigold” because it went into the cooking pot: medieval cooks used the petals in soups and to colour butter and cheese, earning it the nickname “poor man’s saffron.” It is a different plant from the common Tagetes marigold.
